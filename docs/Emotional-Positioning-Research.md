# Emotional Positioning: What the Research Says

Prepared 17 September 2026 for the Distill team. Companion to the Emotional Positioning sheet in Routing-Table.xlsx.

## The one-paragraph version

Every wearable app is built on the same assumption: show someone a bad number and they will behave better. Thirty years of psychology research says that is wrong more often than it is right. Feedback that points at the person makes them worse, guilt after a slip causes a bigger slip, being told what to do makes people argue back, and chasing a daily score makes some people anxious enough to need a sleep clinic. The apps in this category are not failing on data. They are failing on psychology. That gap is the positioning.

## Six findings, in plain words

### 1. Feedback that points at the self makes things worse

Kluger and DeNisi's 1996 review pooled 607 studies of feedback covering more than 23,000 people. On average feedback helped, but more than a third of the time it made performance worse. The pattern behind the failures: when feedback pulls attention toward the person ("your recovery is poor") instead of the task ("sleep was 41 minutes shorter on those nights"), people spend their energy defending their self-image instead of changing what they do.

What we do: every verdict describes the night and the habit. There is no score that describes the person.

### 2. One slip, plus guilt, causes the next slip

Polivy and Herman named the "what-the-hell effect" in the 1970s: once dieters feel they have broken the rule, they abandon the whole day. The same thing happens with alcohol. A 2025 UCL study of people who drink at higher-risk levels found that the worse people felt about last night, the more they drank tonight. Guilt does not correct the behaviour. It fuels the next round.

What we do: the morning after an off-plan night reads exactly like any other morning. The number is shown, the cause is named without comment, nothing is red, and the experiment carries on. An off night is a data point, never a failure.

### 3. Telling ambivalent people what to do makes them argue the other side

Miller and Rollnick's motivational interviewing calls it the "righting reflex": the helper's urge to fix. When a helper pushes one side of a person's ambivalence ("you should cut back"), the person instinctively defends the other side. Across decades of counselling studies, directive and confrontational styles produce more drop-out and relapse, and empathic styles produce better outcomes. Experiments with online health messages find the same thing at small scale: controlling language ("you must") raises resistance, language that offers choice lowers it.

What we do: no "should", "must", or "need to" anywhere in the product. Every verdict ends with the decision in the person's hands. "Your call" is the mechanism, not a softener.

### 4. Self-compassion produces more healthy behaviour, not less

The common worry is that being kind to yourself after a lapse makes you lazy. The evidence says the opposite. Sirois, Kitner and Hirsch pooled 15 samples totalling 3,252 people and found that people who respond to their own failures with kindness do more healthy things, not fewer. The reason is simple: shame and rumination use up the energy that self-control needs. Failure treated as normal keeps people in the game.

What we do: lapses are expected and designed for. "Missed taps are unknown, never failed" is the smallest example. The reset protocol for bad weeks is the largest.

### 5. Praise the process, not the person, and the same goes for criticism

Carol Dweck's work on praise found that "you worked hard" builds persistence and "you're smart" makes people fragile when things get difficult. Negative feedback follows the same rule in reverse. "You were consistent for eight months" is about what they did. "Your sleep is bad" is about who they are.

What we do: effort is named before every verdict. The consistency is acknowledged as effort, and then the verdict is about the item.

### 6. The tracker itself can become the problem

Baron and colleagues coined "orthosomnia" in 2017 after seeing patients arrive at sleep clinics with normal sleep and real distress, caused by chasing their tracker's score. Several stayed anxious even after a lab sleep study showed nothing wrong. Separately, Epstein's research on why people stop tracking found that lapses are a normal part of use and that most apps punish lapses instead of designing for them. About a third of tracker owners stop wearing them within six months.

What we do: no daily score to chase, no dashboard, one line a day, a verdict every two weeks. An option to hide the wearable's own score entirely.

## The morning after drinks, side by side

**What they get today.** A red recovery of 31 percent. A strain warning. A line saying alcohol had a significant negative impact. A suggestion to take it easy. The person feels told off for a good evening with friends, and has learned nothing they did not already know.

**What we show when alcohol is the current experiment.** "Last night: two drinks. Your HRV came in 19 points under your usual, and you were awake 34 minutes more than normal. That's one on-night logged. Twelve to go." No colour. The same tone as any other morning.

**What we show when it isn't.** Nothing about it. It is not being tested, so it is not mentioned. If the night looks unusual, one optional tap: "Anything different last night?" so it can be kept out of the running test.

**What the verdict says two weeks later.** "Alcohol: HRV was 17 points lower and you slept 38 minutes less on drinking nights, against your normal swing of 9 points and 30 minutes. That's the size of it. Now you know what a Tuesday drink costs and what a Saturday one is worth. Yours to spend."

**Why this is positioning and not just copy.** Every other product in the category casts itself as the coach and the user as the one being corrected. This one casts the user as the person deciding and itself as the friend who checked the numbers. "Editing, not deprivation" is the brand, and the morning-after screen is where it is proven or disproven.

## Eight rules for every screen

1. Describe the night, not the person.
2. Nothing is ever red.
3. Name the cause, skip the comment.
4. Effort first, then verdict.
5. Choice at the end of every sentence.
6. A lapse is a data point, not a failure.
7. Verdicts on a fortnight, not a morning.
8. The scoreboard goes down.

## Where this could go wrong

- **Too soft reads as evasive.** The self-compassion research works because the facts are still delivered. A verdict that hides the number to spare feelings breaks trust faster than a harsh one. The number is always shown.
- **"Your call" can become a cop-out.** It has to sit next to a real, specific finding. "Your call" after "inconclusive, and here is the exact size we saw" is honest. "Your call" after nothing is abdication.
- **One study is not a law.** A few of the health-app experiments on autonomy-supportive language found no difference between wording styles. The direction of the evidence is clear, but the effect sizes are small. The strongest findings here are the feedback review and the self-compassion pooled analysis, and those are the ones to lean on.
